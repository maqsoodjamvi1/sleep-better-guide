If you have ever struggled to fall asleep, you have probably received a mountain of unsolicited advice. Friends, wellness influencers, and random internet forums love to tell you how to fix your nights. The problem is that a lot of standard sleep advice comes from outdated rules that haven’t evolved in decades.

We live in a world of high-definition screens, variable work hours, and high-stress environments. Treating your bedroom like a strict, joyless medical ward isn’t just unrealistic—it often creates performance anxiety that keeps you awake.

Over the last few years, I decided to test my own routines. I stopped following the rigid sleep dogma that made me feel like a failure every time I slipped up. It turns out that letting go of these five outdated rules didn’t wreck my sleep; it actually made it much better.

Rule 1: You Must Be in Bed by 10:00 PM Every Single Night

The old guard of sleep hygiene treats the clock like a strict tyrant. The narrative goes that if you aren’t horizontal and turning off the lights by 10:00 PM, you are somehow ruining your circadian rhythm.

In reality, sleep architecture is far more flexible than that. If you force yourself into bed when your body is wide awake, you will likely spend an hour staring at the ceiling, which conditions your brain to associate your bed with frustration rather than rest.

The practical fix: Focus on your wake-up time instead of your bedtime. Pick a realistic time to get up in the morning and stick to it most days of the week. If you aren’t tired at 10:00 PM, stay up reading or doing a low-key activity until your eyelids actually feel heavy. Your natural sleep drive will eventually catch up with you.

Rule 2: Zero Screens for Two Hours Before Bed

We have all heard the stern warning: ban all phones, tablets, and televisions at least two hours before sleep because blue light destroys melatonin production. While the science behind blue light is real, the two-hour total ban is wildly impractical for modern adults, and frankly, it often backfires.

When you try to go cold turkey on screens, you often end up staring into space, feeling bored, and scrolling on your phone anyway out of sheer habit, which then triggers guilt.

The practical fix: Stop worrying about every single photon of light and focus on content and distance instead. Keep your phone at arm’s length so you aren’t tempted to doom-scroll social media feeds. Switch your device to its warmest color setting, turn down the brightness, and watch a familiar sitcom or read an e-reader. It is much better to wind down with a comforting, low-stakes video than to lie in the dark stressing about how you aren’t sleeping.

Rule 3: If You Wake Up at Night, Stay in Bed and Do Breathing Exercises

For years, the conventional wisdom dictated that getting out of bed when you woke up in the middle of the night was a cardinal sin. You were supposed to stay put, keep your eyes closed, and force yourself to breathe deeply until you drifted back off.

For me, this rule resulted in hours of agonizing wakefulness. Staying in bed while your mind is racing creates a feedback loop of anxiety.

The practical fix: Utilize the twenty-minute rule. If you wake up and cannot fall back asleep within roughly twenty minutes, get out of bed. Go to a dimly lit room, sit on a comfortable chair, and do something monotonous like folding laundry, reading a dull instruction manual, or sketching. Do not turn on bright overhead lights or check work emails. Once your eyes start to sting with fatigue, head back to bed.

Rule 4: Your Bedroom Must Be Completely Silent and Pitch Black

Walk into any idealized sleep sanctuary on the internet, and you will see blackout curtains, heavy storm-proof windows, and total silence. While this is great in theory, striving for absolute sensory deprivation can make you hyper-sensitive to every tiny creak in your house or light leak under the door.

When you train your brain to require absolute silence and darkness, normal neighborhood noises—like a car driving by or a neighbor closing a door—will shock you fully awake.

The practical fix: Build some ambient resilience. Introduce a predictable, low-frequency sound like a box fan or a white noise machine to mask sudden sharp noises. If you like a little bit of light, use a amber-toned nightlight in the hallway. You want your sleep environment to be comfortable, not fragile.

Rule 5: A Bad Night of Sleep Means Your Next Day is Ruined

Perhaps the most damaging rule of all is the mental story we tell ourselves the morning after a rough night. We wake up, look at the clock, realize we only got four hours of sleep, and immediately think, “Today is going to be a disaster.”

This catastrophic thinking releases cortisol and adrenaline, which actually do ruin your day, far more than the physical sleep deficit itself.

The practical fix: Separate how you feel from how you can function. It is entirely possible to have a productive workday, enjoy a workout, and have a nice dinner even when you are operating on subpar rest. Accept the fatigue without dramatizing it. Drop the heavy expectations for the day, keep moving, and trust that your body’s homeostatic sleep pressure will help you sleep much easier the following night.

If you are ready to stop stressing over rigid routines and want to find a sustainable approach that fits your actual lifestyle, taking a closer look at what works for your specific schedule is the best place to start.
